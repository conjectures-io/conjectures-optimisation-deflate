From 28 September to 12 October 2026, miners on the subnet competed on one small, old
and heavily used piece of software: the parser at the heart of DEFLATE, the format behind
gzip, zlib, PNG and ZIP. A submission was two files. One was a Rust function that reads a
buffer and writes LZ77 tokens: a literal byte, or "copy this many bytes from that far
back". The other was a Lean proof that the tokens decode back to the input, for every
possible input. The validator checked the proof, compiled the parser, ran it on a public
corpus and a held-out twin, and paid the Pareto frontier of compressed size against time.

We wanted to answer two questions at the end. Which of these parsers are good in general,
not just on the files they were scored on? And how do they compare with the DEFLATE
implementations people actually use?

## What a submission could and could not do

The proof covers only the places where a parser writes a token: every match it emits
must point at bytes that really match. The search behind it, which is where all the
speed and compression come from, is unconstrained. Hash chains, binary trees, lazy
matching and full dynamic-programming "optimal" parses all appeared, and the proof for
each was a few hundred lines of Lean.

Everything after the parse was fixed. A shared encoder turned tokens into DEFLATE blocks
of exactly 16,384 tokens with optimal length-limited Huffman codes. Fixing it kept the
contest about the parse, which matters later: block splitting is where production
encoders like libdeflate earn part of their advantage, and nobody here was allowed to
compete on it.

## The frontier grew, and so did the tuning

The frontier went from 7 points on the first day to 101 on the last. Almost no two
submissions behaved identically on every file (654 reruns formed 647 distinct groups),
so this was not mass resubmission. It was many parsers each holding a thin slice of the
time axis.

The scored corpora were a public set and a held-out set cut from the same pool of
sources. That design catches a miner who memorizes the public files. It cannot catch a
miner who tunes to the pool, because the held-out set is its twin: the two rankings
agreed at a rank correlation of 0.998 from start to finish.

A third corpus built from unrelated sources could, and did. The rank correlation between
the public corpus and that out-of-distribution set fell from 0.977 on 29 September to
0.961 on 2 October and to 0.759 by 12 October. Parsers that detect content classes, skip
positions after a missed match, or hash four bytes instead of three gained on the scored
files and collapsed on others: 68% worse than the baseline on Android DEX integer tables,
492% worse on a Windows event log.

## How we tested for "good in general"

We built a fresh holdout after the fact: 905 files, 50.5 MiB, sampled from seven kinds of
real data that no miner was scored on. Web assets from npm releases and the Python
documentation, slices of large files (Silesia, Wikipedia, a Linux tarball), genomics
(VCF, FASTQ, BAM records), the filtered scanlines inside PNG images, archive members
(CPython's standard library, a Java JAR), our own servers' logs, and JSON event messages.
We kept it private and did not run anything on it until we had written down, and
committed, how we would choose.

We also built a robustness set: tiny inputs, degenerate ones (zeros, short periods),
data that repeats just beyond the 32 KiB window, de Bruijn sequences where every
three-byte sequence repeats but no four-byte one does, integer tables, UTF-16 text,
Fibonacci and Thue–Morse strings, and the same text at 1, 4, 16 and 64 MiB.

Then we ran every candidate on one pinned core of the machine that ran the competition's
reruns, interleaved with zlib, zlib-ng, miniz_oxide and libdeflate at several levels.
Sizes are relative to the competition's baseline parser and averaged per category, so
neither a big file nor a format with many files can dominate.

One honest note on method. Two of our pre-registered robustness rules turned out to
measure the shared encoder rather than the parsers. One of them failed every method on
random data, including the baseline and every reference library. We recorded the change
and its reason before the final selection, and both versions of the screen appear in the
technical report.

## What fresh data says about the record-holders

The most recent submissions at the top of the official leaderboard did not survive
contact with new data.

- The parser with the smallest official output, submitted on 11 October, comes out at
  0.996 of the baseline's size on the fresh holdout. It keeps 7% of the gain it showed
  on the scored corpora, and on logs it is 11% worse than the baseline.
- The four best-on-the-public-corpus submissions from 11 and 12 October produce larger
  output than a submission from 2 October, at three and a half times its running time.
- A lineage that began with a 30 September submission compresses 71% worse than the
  baseline on a 64 MiB input while being fine at 16 MiB. The current record-holders
  inherited it. Nothing in the competition was larger than 1.5 MB, so nobody could see it.
- The fastest point on the official frontier is 33% larger than the baseline on the
  holdout. One of the frontier submissions whose source was withheld writes our logs at
  18 times the baseline's size.

None of this breaks a proof. Every one of these parsers is still correct on every input.
Correctness was proved; compression and speed were only measured, and measured on a
narrow distribution.

## Five parsers that hold up

We screened 51 candidates (every group on the general frontier, the record-holders, the
fastest and the withheld frontier points) and kept the ones that never lost more than a
fifth of the baseline's size on any file that mattered, never slowed down
pathologically, and scaled linearly to 64 MiB. Eighteen passed. From the frontier of
those on the fresh holdout, the pre-registered rule picked five, from fastest to
smallest:

- **#55** (29 September): 1% smaller than the baseline at 0.61 times its time.
- **#232** (2 October): 4.5% smaller at 1.1 times.
- **#657** (12 October): 5% smaller at 1.4 times.
- **#355** (5 October): 6.4% smaller at 2.5 times.
- **#243** (2 October): 6.9% smaller at 3.6 times.

Four of the five were submitted before the overfitting wave of 7 and 8 October. #243 has
the best average compression on the out-of-distribution corpus of any submission, before
or since.

## Next to the libraries people use

On the fresh holdout, with the competition's own encoder, the honest picture has three
parts.

- **Up to about three quarters of the baseline's time, libdeflate wins.** Its level 6 is
  smaller and faster than every fast miner submission, and its level 9 (5.4% smaller than
  the baseline at 0.73 times its time) beats three of our five selected parsers on both
  axes. The gap is the price of the provable subset of Rust: byte-by-byte comparison
  loops, bounds checks, no word-at-a-time matching. Turning off overflow checks, which the
  proofs make redundant, changes nothing measurable.
- **From about 1.6 to 3.6 times the baseline's time, the miners' parsers are the best
  option measured.** #85, #355 and #243 are on average 1.1 to 1.6% smaller than
  libdeflate-9 and two to four times faster than libdeflate-12. They are also 1.7 to 2.3%
  smaller than zlib level 9 and 2.8 to 3.3% smaller than miniz_oxide level 9. At about the
  speed of zlib-9, #657 matches its size.
- **libdeflate's level 12 produces the smallest output, but not because of its parse.**
  Feed libdeflate-12's own tokens through the competition's encoder and the result is
  larger than #243's on the competition corpus. The difference is the block splitter. We
  added adaptive block splitting to our harness (cut where the symbol statistics change,
  keep a cut only if the exact cost drops). It makes every parse 0.7 to 0.9% smaller, and
  libdeflate-12's tokens through it come out smaller than libdeflate-12 itself.

## Seven real-world jobs

Last, we put the selected parsers to work, unchanged, on seven jobs DEFLATE does every day.
An adapter calls each one on "the last 32 KiB plus the new data", keeps only the tokens
past the history, and trims a match that straddles the boundary, so a whole-buffer parser
can stream. The parsers got adaptive block splitting, as any production encoder has. Every
output was decoded by zlib, miniz_oxide and libdeflate before it counted.

- **Static web assets, precompressed once** (npm releases and the Python documentation).
  #243 matches libdeflate-12 and lands within 0.5% of zopfli, the usual tool for this job,
  at 29 times zopfli's speed. #355 and #657 give up 0.1 to 0.4% of that for two to three
  and a half times more speed. Web assets leave only about 2.5% over zlib level 6 for
  anyone.
- **Large files, streamed in 1 MiB chunks** (Silesia, Wikipedia, source tarballs). Every
  selected parser except the fastest is 3.4 to 4.1% smaller than `gzip -9`. #232, #657
  and #521 manage it at equal or better speed. #243 is smaller than 7-Zip's deflate at
  3.4 times its speed.
- **Logs, streamed with a flush every 64 KiB.** The biggest win anywhere: #355 and #85
  write our servers' logs 14 to 15% smaller than zlib-6 and 9% smaller than zlib-ng. At 27
  to 38 MB/s against zlib-ng's 300, that is a job for log archiving, not for a log
  shipper's hot path.
- **Archive members, one stream per file** (CPython's standard library, a Java JAR). #243
  and #355 tie libdeflate-12, #355 at more than twice its speed.
- **Genomics in BGZF's 64 KiB blocks** (VCF, FASTQ, BAM records). #355 is 6% smaller than
  zlib-6, but libdeflate-12 is another 2.5% smaller: a solid result, not a headline.
- **PNG image data.** Not a job for these parsers. zopflipng and oxipng, which also choose
  filters, are about 4% smaller than the best of them, and libdeflate-12 alone is 3%
  smaller. No
  parser was ever tuned on filtered scanlines.
- **One small message at a time, with a flush after each** (WebSocket style). Not a job
  for a one-shot parser either. With a 32 KiB window they are 3% smaller than zlib but 7
  to 45 times slower, because the history is parsed again for every message. With a
  4 KiB window they are 40% larger, because a parser that does not know the window picks
  matches the window cannot reach.

Large files also caught one more defect. Two of the five selected parsers, #355 and #657,
compress normally up to about 32 and 50 MB in a single buffer and then degrade, by up to
36% and 51% on a 268 MB input. Streamed in chunks, they are fine. Our robustness set
stopped at 64 MB, which they passed. #243, #85, #232 and #521 hold at every size we tried.

### The few worth keeping

- **#243** (2 October): the maximum-compression choice for static assets, archives and
  large files. libdeflate-12's size there, zopfli within half a percent, robust at every
  size.
- **#85** (29 September): nearly the same compression as #355 at a little more speed, and
  the only high-compression parser that passes even the strictest version of our screen.
- **#355** (5 October): the best of them on logs and genomics, but only behind the
  streaming adapter.
- **#521** (8 October), **#657** (12 October) and **#232** (2 October): the balanced
  choices, 3 to 4% under `gzip -9` at its speed or better.

The fastest selected parser, #55, is at best level with zlib-6 and slower than zlib-ng or
libdeflate in every job, which is what the fast end of the competition looked like from
the start.

## What we would do differently

- **Hold out something different, not a twin.** A held-out copy of the scored
  distribution protects against memorization and nothing else. Rotate genuinely different
  private corpora, and never publish their per-submission aggregates. Ours became a
  second public scoreboard.
- **Score the tails.** Include tiny files and files far larger than the corpus maximum,
  and add a do-no-harm rule per file. Average-only scoring paid parsers that lose 70% on
  one format.
- **Fix the scoring hardware in the rules.** Re-timing on a different machine moved a
  third of the pay.
- **Let someone compete on the encoder, or give everyone adaptive blocks.** Fixed blocks
  kept the contest clean, but they hide almost a percent that any production use gets
  back, and they make the comparison with libraries look worse than the parsers are.
- **Proofs and benchmarks answer different questions.** The proof made every one of
  these 648 parsers safe to run on anything. It took a different kind of test to find
  out which of them are worth running.
